Results

Table 1 summarizes real (CPI-deflated, 2022 rupees) median earnings growth by ISCO-08 sub-major group between 2022 and 2025, sorted by AI/LLM exposure score. Groups 71, 92, and 93 are excluded: median cash earnings equal zero in both years for these groups, reflecting high rates of unpaid family labor and subsistence work, which makes percentage growth undefined.

The four most exposed occupation groups in the sample are all clerical support roles, three of which show real median earnings declines of 7 to 12 percent. ICT professionals, the next most exposed group, show earnings growth close to zero rather than the pronounced gains a purely mean-based comparison would suggest -- the group's mean growth (+5.6%) is pulled upward by earners at the top of its distribution, not shared broadly across the occupation.

An occupation-group-level OLS regression of median earnings growth on exposure (n = 35, following exclusion of the three undefined-growth groups) finds a negative bivariate relationship (beta = -8.58, robust SE = 4.66, p = 0.074) that does not survive the inclusion of demographic controls -- percent female, mean age, percent urban, and mean education level (beta = -6.94, SE = 6.78, p = 0.315). A Random Forest fit on the same 35 observations ranks exposure as the least important of five predictors (permutation importance 0.029, versus 0.485 for mean age) and produces a leave-one-out cross-validated R-squared of -0.029, indicating no genuine predictive power at this sample size. Group mean age and exposure are themselves correlated (r = -0.38): more exposed occupations tend to employ younger workers, and this confound likely accounts for age dominating the group-level analysis.

Because 35 group-level observations provide limited statistical power, we turn to a person-level specification using the full pooled PLFS sample (n = 400,115, after excluding unpaid family workers and non-positive earners):

log(earnings) = b0 + b1(exposure x post2025) + b2(exposure) + b3(post2025) + controls + e

with standard errors clustered by ISCO-08 sub-major group (38 clusters) to account for the fact that exposure is measured at the group level rather than varying across individuals. The coefficient of interest, the exposure x post2025 interaction, is negative and statistically significant under conventional cluster-robust inference (beta = -0.069, clustered SE = 0.0295, p = 0.025), implying that a one-unit increase in exposure is associated with roughly 6.9 percentage points less real earnings growth over the period, holding age, sex, education, and urban/rural location constant. Uncorrected (non-clustered) standard errors understate this uncertainty substantially, producing p ~ 0.000 -- a version of the Moulton problem that arises whenever the covariate of interest varies at a coarser level than the unit of observation.

Given that 38 clusters sits near the lower bound of where standard cluster-robust asymptotics are considered reliable, we additionally ran a wild cluster bootstrap (Cameron, Gelbach & Miller 2008; 999 replications, Rademacher weights, null imposed on the restricted model). This more conservative test returns p = 0.097 -- outside conventional significance at the 5% level, though still notably below what pure chance would produce. We report this discrepancy directly rather than selecting the more favorable of the two p-values: the asymptotic cluster-robust result and the bootstrap result do not agree, and the honest reading of the evidence is that the exposure-earnings-growth relationship is suggestive rather than conclusively established at this sample size.

Taken together, four independent pieces of evidence -- the sorted descriptive pattern, the sign and marginal significance of the bivariate group-level regression, the negative and larger-magnitude person-level coefficient, and a bootstrap p-value that, while not reaching 0.05, remains low -- point in the same direction: routine, clerical occupations with high LLM exposure show weaker real earnings growth than less exposed or more skill-intensive occupations over 2022-2025. We treat this as a credible but provisional finding, consistent with a substitution-biased effect of LLM adoption concentrated in routine task-intensive work, and note that a larger panel or additional survey waves would be needed to establish it with the reliability conventional significance thresholds are meant to signal.

Discussion

The central finding -- that high-LLM-exposure clerical occupations show weaker real earnings growth than less-exposed or more skill-intensive occupations over 2022-2025 -- is consistent across four independent checks, but several methodological choices made along the way shape how much weight that finding can bear.

Occupation crosswalk coverage. Exposure scores originate at the O*NET-SOC level from the Global Automation Atlas, bridged to India through a chain of two crosswalks: SOC 2010 to ISCO-08 (official BLS correspondence), and NCO-2015 to ISCO-08 (2-digit truncation, justified by NCO-2015's documented structural alignment with ISCO-08 at the major/sub-major group level). The SOC-ISCO step initially matched only 85% of India's 923 occupation titles, rising to 88.4% after a manually documented override recovered ICT professionals -- an entire occupation family absent from the crosswalk only because SOC 2010 renumbered "Computer Occupations" under SOC 2018. Because ICT professionals are arguably the occupation group most central to a study of LLM exposure, leaving this gap unaddressed would have biased the analysis away from its own subject matter; the override is documented in the pipeline code rather than applied silently. At the person-record level, final coverage reached 96.5%, with the remaining gap concentrated in two low-relevance groups (subsistence farmers, street vendors).

Earnings measure and sample restrictions. Occupation groups 71, 92, and 93 were dropped from the group-level analysis because median cash earnings equal zero in both survey years -- a real feature of India's labor market (high rates of unpaid family labor and subsistence work in these occupations) rather than a data error, but one that means the analysis is silent on wage dynamics for a substantial share of workers in building trades, agriculture, and manual labor. At the person level, the regression sample excludes unpaid family workers and non-positive earners, a further restriction from 500,529 to 400,115 records (about 20%). This is standard practice for log-earnings specifications, but it means the reported coefficient describes earnings growth among paid workers specifically, not overall economic welfare across the full employed population.

Mean-versus-median sensitivity. An early version of the group-level analysis, built on mean earnings, suggested ICT professionals saw strong positive real growth (+5.6%). Switching to the median -- more robust to the composition differences that arise from PLFS 2022 and 2025 being independent cross-sections rather than a panel -- revealed this was driven by a small number of high earners in the 2025 sample rather than broad-based gains; the median-based growth for ICT professionals is closer to flat (+0.9%). This instance suggests the mean-based versions of other unexamined group comparisons in adjacent literature may be similarly vulnerable to compositional noise, and argues for median-based reporting as the more conservative default in cross-sectional occupation studies.

Unweighted exposure aggregation. The India-specific exposure score for each ISCO-08 group is an unweighted mean across the O*NET-SOC occupations mapped into it. This treats a narrow, low-employment occupation title the same as a high-employment one when computing a group's exposure level, which may misstate the effective exposure actually faced by the average worker in that group. An employment-weighted aggregation, using PLFS's own occupation frequencies as weights, would be a natural refinement for future work.

Statistical power and inference. The group-level regression (n = 35 after exclusions) lacks the power to detect an exposure effect independent of occupation-group demographics; the Random Forest's negative leave-one-out R-squared confirms this directly rather than leaving it to inference from a small coefficient. The person-level specification restores power by using individual variation, but the exposure variable itself still varies at only 38 clusters, which sits at the low end of where standard cluster-robust asymptotics are considered dependable. The wild cluster bootstrap's weaker result (p = 0.097 against an asymptotic p = 0.025) is the clearest single piece of evidence that this analysis should be read as identifying a plausible, consistent pattern rather than a definitively established effect. A longer panel -- additional PLFS waves, or administrative data with more occupation-group variation -- would be the most direct way to resolve this in future work.

None of these limitations invalidate the pattern documented in the Results section. They do mean the paper's contribution should be framed accordingly: as evidence consistent with substitution-biased effects of LLM exposure on routine occupations in India, established through a transparent and reproducible pipeline, rather than as a definitive causal estimate.
